# What Comes After Z?

_Published: 2026-05-27_

Demonstrations spearheaded by young activists [spread to nearly every continent](https://www.journalofdemocracy.org/articles/why-gen-z-is-rising/), beginning in Kenya and Bangladesh in 2024 and expanding to Nepal, Indonesia, Madagascar, Peru, Bulgaria and beyond. Each movement was typically sparked by a corruption scandal or unpopular policy proposal. While the [ideological alignment](https://www.lemonde.fr/en/international/article/2025/11/22/the-ambiguities-of-the-generation-z-movement-in-mexico_6747717_4.html) of each movement varied, a general trend of anti-corruption demands and [organizing via social media](https://www.idea.int/blog/democracy-goes-digital-social-media-and-bulgarias-gen-z-protests) united them. Indeed, protestors across the globe saw similarities in their movements, [adopting a single banner](https://www.npr.org/2025/10/05/nx-s1-5560980/gen-z-protesters-one-piece-pirate-flag) and sharing their experiences online. Bangladesh, one of the first countries where protests made a splash, and Bulgaria, one of the last, both held elections early this year, shedding light on the electoral dynamics of these movements. Bangladesh was a harbinger of what would become an international trend. Beginning in June 2024, a series of student-led protests began, sparked by anger over the reinstatement of a job quota that would have reserved 30% of government posts for the children of freedom fighters in the country’s 1971 independence war. Protesters [alleged these quotas](https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/why-are-bangladesh-students-protesting-against-job-quotas-2024-07-18/) would further cement the power of the Awami League, the party of then-Prime Minister Sheikh Hasina that had led the independence war. Hasina’s time as prime minister was [marked by consistent democratic backsliding](https://oxfordpoliticalreview.com/2025/04/04/sheikh-hasinas-rule-in-bangladesh-the-road-to-authoritarianism/) and consolidation of power in her and her party’s hands. Violent repression of the protests ensued, but quickly backfired on Hasina’s government, driving more people into the streets. Brutal [violations of fundamental rights](https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2024/07/what-is-happening-at-the-quota-reform-protests-in-bangladesh/) led to the deaths of an estimated [1,400 protesters](https://www.ohchr.org/en/press-releases/2025/02/bangladesh-un-report-finds-brutal-systematic-repression-protests-calls) that summer. On August 5, 2024, the military refused orders to shoot unarmed protestors near the prime minister’s residence, culminating in Hasina fleeing to India and ending her 15-year tenure as Bangladeshi prime minister. The caretaker government installed after Hasina’s ouster included representatives from major student movements. Despite [high hopes](https://www.crisisgroup.org/qna/asia-pacific/bangladesh/curtain-falls-bangladeshs-interim-government-critical-elections-approach) that this new coalition could pass major reforms ahead of elections, it was [only able to deliver on some](https://www.crisisgroup.org/brf/asia-pacific/bangladesh/b187-bangladeshs-new-government-gets-down-business) of its promises due to bureaucratic resistance and the inability to achieve political consensus. Nevertheless, one of its major achievements was the drafting of [the July Charter](https://constitutionnet.org/vl/item/july-national-charter-2025-bangladesh-english-translation), a blueprint for future reforms, [approved in a referendum](https://www.bsg.ox.ac.uk/blog/bangladeshs-2026-national-election-and-referendum-what-comes-next) held concurrently with the February 2026 elections organized by the interim government. And yet, while the reform package was approved with 68% of the votes, the National Citizen Party (NCP), formed by students ahead of the elections, won only six seats in parliament.
